# Queued: visual and experience redesign

*Received 2026-11-26. Start only after the first version is launched.*

## Device targets
Designed for the iPhone 17 family and most Android phones (narrow ~360 px wide through large ~440 px wide), with safe areas, notches and gesture bars handled.

## The brief (user's words)

Redesign the look and feel of my meal planning app. Keep every
feature and all saved data working exactly as they do now. This is
a visual and experience overhaul, not a rebuild.

WHO IT'S FOR
My wife is the main user. Design for her as a person with taste,
not for "women" as a category. She should open it and feel it was
made with care: calm, warm, beautiful, quietly clever. A man
picking it up should feel just as at home. So no pink by default,
script fonts, florals, hearts, or "mama" language, and equally no
cold, techy, gadget feel.
Her taste: [Instagram, Pinterest, Half Baked Harvest cookbook]

THE FEELING
A favorite cookbook crossed with a well-kept kitchen, not
software. Food is the star: big appetizing photos and real
ingredient colors do the decorating. Planning the week should
feel like a pleasant ten-minute ritual, not admin.

WHAT "NO SAAS" MEANS
No dashboards, stat tiles, progress rings, streaks, or badges. No
sidebar icon navigation, grids of identical rounded cards with
grey shadows, gradient banners, upgrade prompts, onboarding
tours, or blobby illustrations. No productivity words like
"optimize," "workflow," or "manage." Use words you'd say in a
kitchen.

VISUAL DIRECTION
- Build the palette from food and kitchen materials (produce,
  linen, ceramic, wood, copper), not a UI kit. Avoid the stock
  "cream background, serif headline, terracotta accent" look
  that every AI design lands on.
- One or two typefaces with real character, a clear size scale,
  generous spacing, comfortable reading sizes.
- Let layout follow content: a week should look like a week, a
  recipe like a recipe page, a shopping list like a tidy
  handwritten list.
- Spend the boldness in one place, a signature element she'd
  show a friend, and keep everything else quiet.

EXPERIENCE
- She uses it on her phone, often one-handed and interrupted, at
  the counter and in the store. Big touch targets, main action
  within thumb reach, nothing lost if she's pulled away mid-task.
- Find the three things she does most (likely: see this week,
  swap a meal, check the shopping list) and make each one or two
  taps from opening the app.
- Motion only where it answers her touch: a meal settling into a
  day, an item crossing off. No decorative animation.
- Thoughtful touches over new features: plain friendly wording,
  empty screens that suggest a next step, nothing that nags or
  guilt-trips.
- Readable contrast, large-text friendly, and a dark mode that's
  designed rather than inverted.

HOW TO WORK
1. Go through the existing app first. Tell me what each screen
   does today and what feels generic about it.
2. Show me three clearly different directions as polished mockups
   of the same screen (the weekly plan), each with its palette,
   typefaces, and a one-line rationale. Throw out any direction
   you'd also have produced for a fitness or budgeting app.
3. When I pick one, restyle that single screen fully and show
   phone-size screenshots, light and dark.
4. Only after I approve, roll the look out to the other screens
   with before/after screenshots of each.

## Notes for whoever picks this up
- The taste references set a mood only. Don't copy any cookbook's or brand's actual design.
- Food photos must be real. Plan the source before the directions round: the recipe page's own image (credited), the household's own photos, or a licensed photo service. No AI-generated or illustrated stand-ins.
- The v1 budget meter and sale badges conflict with "no progress rings, badges, stat tiles." The redesign has to express budget and savings in kitchen language without dropping the features.
- Screenshots at iPhone 17 size plus one common Android size, light and dark, taken in two browsers at once.
